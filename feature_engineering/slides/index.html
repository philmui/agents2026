Right Past

How to build features, handle time, and store them
with Chronon and UCI Online Retail.

One decision

Will this customer buy again within 30 days?

One firm rule

Use only facts known before the prediction.

Use ← and → to navigate · press F for fullscreen

Start with the big picture. A machine learning model is a program that learns patterns from examples and then makes predictions about new cases. To make a prediction it needs inputs, and each input is called a feature. A feature store is the shared system that builds those features and hands them to the model. The whole deck is organized around one honest question we keep coming back to: could the model really have known this fact at the moment it made its decision? Our running example is a real online shop, and the one thing we predict is whether a customer will come back and buy again within 30 days. The single rule to remember, shown on the right, is that a model may only use facts that were already known before the prediction.

Here is the map of the whole talk in four moves. First, set the time: decide the exact moment the model makes its call, because everything else is measured against that instant. Second, build the features: write down, one time, the rule that turns raw events into an input number. Third, check the result: confirm the numbers used for training match the numbers served to the live model, and prove no future information leaked in. Fourth, take responsibility: give every feature a named owner, a version, and a record of where it came from, so that when something breaks there is a person to call. If a student remembers only these four moves, they have the shape of the entire field.

Important: a fast database is just storage. It becomes a feature store only when a team defines, tests, and owns the features.

A common misunderstanding is that a feature store is just a fast database. It is not. Read the row left to right, because it shows the four stages that actually make it a feature store. It starts with raw events, the things that happened, like orders and refunds. Then come the time rules, which say which key to group by (here, the customer), how far back to look, and where to draw the cutoff. Then a feature contract, which is the written agreement about what the feature means, who owns it, its version, and where it came from. Finally delivery, which sends the exact same values to both training and the live service. A database gives you only the storage. It becomes a feature store when a team adds the meaning, the tests, and the ownership around it.

We learn on real data, not a tidy toy example, because real data is where the interesting mistakes hide. This is a British online gift shop's sales log from late 2010 to late 2011. Point out the numbers that shape our work. There are 541,909 rows, but a row is one product line, not one order, so a single order with five items shows up as five rows. Nearly a quarter of the rows have no customer ID, probably guest checkouts, and we cannot attach those to anyone's history. Returns are marked by an invoice ID that starts with the letter C. Every one of these quirks forces a decision, and making those decisions carefully, instead of quietly deleting rows, is a large part of honest feature engineering.

The most common beginner mistake is to open the data and ask "what can I compute?" Do the opposite: start by writing down the decision in plain words, and let that dictate everything else. Read the four rows on the left as one sentence. The entity is the thing we are describing, one customer. The decision event is the moment we act, a finished order. The cutoff is the timestamp on that order, the line between past and future. The label is the answer we are trying to predict, whether that customer buys again within 30 days. The card on the right captures the mindset for every single training row: freeze time at the order and ask only what we honestly knew at that instant. Nail down the decision and the rest of the design almost falls out of it.

Caution: never count a negative quantity or a C invoice as a purchase, or a customer who returned everything looks like your best one.

Raw data almost never arrives in the shape you want, so the first real step is to reshape it. We take the messy invoice lines and turn them into three clean tables. Orders is one row per finished purchase, with its total value. Cancellations is one row per return or refund. Predictions is one row per decision we want to score, which for us lines up with each finished order. Doing this cleanup once, in a way that produces the exact same tables every time, matters because a feature you cannot reproduce is a feature you cannot debug. The caution at the bottom is a real trap: a return is stored as a negative quantity, so if you accidentally treat it as a purchase, a customer who sent everything back can look like your biggest spender.
The loop

One rule feeds both training and serving.

One shared rule
Walk the loop out loud. Order events arrive, one written rule turns them into a feature such as "money spent in the last 30 days", and that same rule produces two things: the values used to train the model, and the values served to the live model. The last step compares those two sets of values. If you skip the comparison, the two sides can drift apart without anyone noticing, and the model you tested is not the model your customers meet. That gap has a name later in the deck: training-serving skew, which is any difference between the inputs used in training and the inputs used in production.
Time rules

Every event has three clocks.

02 · Time
The three clocks are event time, which is when something actually happened; availability time, which is when your pipeline could first see it and use it; and prediction time, which is the moment the model made, or would have made, a decision. Work the picture: a customer spends £42 at 09:30, the model has to answer at 10:00, and that order only lands in your table at 10:10. Its event time is before the prediction, so a careless query will include it, but it was not available yet. Include it and your training data quietly contains a fact the live system could not have had. You would notice this in production as a model that scored well offline and then performs worse than expected on real traffic.
Looking back honestly

Every decision gets its own window.

What was known then
A point-in-time join is a lookup that returns only what was known at each past prediction time, and the window is the stretch of history it is allowed to look at. Try it with numbers. A customer spends £10 on 1 January, £20 on 10 January, and £999 on 11 January. If the decision you are scoring happened on 10 January, the correct 30-day total is £30, not £1,029. The £999 comes later, so it belongs to the label side of the timeline, not the feature side. Notice that the answer changes for every row, because each row has its own cutoff. That is why you cannot compute one total per customer and reuse it everywhere.
Leakage exposed

Today's total is not the old total.

Recompute test
The tempting shortcut is to join each training row to the customer's current totals, because that query is short and fast. It is also wrong, because today's total already contains everything the customer did after the decision you are scoring. That is data leakage: the answer sneaks into the inputs. Here is the test that catches it. Run the same query again next month. A point-in-time join gives you the same numbers as before, because history did not change. The latest-value join gives you different numbers every time you run it, because the "latest" value keeps moving. A feature that will not reproduce is a feature you cannot debug or trust.
Boundary rule

Say exactly which events count.

Half-open window

FEATURE WINDOW

cutoff - 30d ≤ event < cutoff

LABEL WINDOW

cutoff < repeat ≤ cutoff + 30d

An event at the exact cutoff?

Leave it out unless you know the true order.

Arrived ten minutes late?

Going by event time alone still leaks.

A half-open window includes one end and excludes the other, and the two formulas on the slide say which end. The feature window runs from 30 days before the cutoff up to the cutoff but stops just short of it. The label window starts just after the cutoff and runs 30 days forward. Written that way, no event can land in both, so a fact can never be an input and an answer at the same time. The awkward case is an event stamped at exactly the cutoff. You usually cannot tell whether it happened a moment before or a moment after the decision, so exclude it: one row too few is a small loss, but one leaked row teaches the model to cheat.

Counterexample: store the current cart as history and your copy is out of date within seconds, and can attach to the wrong key.

Not every fact belongs in the feature store, and knowing what to leave out is as important as knowing what to put in. The split is about time. On the left are summaries of the past, like how much a customer spent in the last 30 days. These take real work to compute from history, they are reused across decisions, and they belong in the store. On the right is the request context: facts that arrive with the request itself and are already known, like the value of the order sitting in front of you right now, or which offer is active. There is no reason to precompute those; just pass them straight to the model. A quick test: if the value would be stale a second from now, it is context, not a stored feature.
How Chronon thinks

Write the meaning down once.

03 · Build
Chronon splits the job into four named pieces, and the next three slides show the code for the first three. A Source says which table and stream to read and which column holds the event time. A GroupBy says which key to group by, what to compute, and over which windows. A Join attaches those computed features to a list of past decisions. Then delivery hands the same values to training and to the live service. The point of naming the pieces is that the meaning lives in one definition instead of being retyped in a training script and again in a serving service, which is how the two ever end up disagreeing.

This is the first of three code slides, and none of them require you to write code fluently, only to read the meaning off each line. A Source tells Chronon where the raw events live and how to read their time. Walk the three cards. The table is the full stored history, used for training. The topic is the live feed of new events, used to keep serving fresh, and having both is how one definition can drive both paths. The time_column names the single column that says when each event happened, and picking the right time column is the whole game, because that is the clock everything is measured against. The selects line just lists the raw fields we care about. Nothing here computes a feature yet; this step only points at trustworthy inputs.

v1 = GroupBy(
    sources=[source],
    keys=["customer_id"],

        operation=Operation.SUM,
        windows=WINDOWS,
    )],
    accuracy=Accuracy.TEMPORAL,
    online=True,
)
If the Source said where to read, the GroupBy says what to compute. Read it as a sentence: group the events by customer_id, and for each customer add up order_value over the last 7, 30, and 90 days. "Aggregation" is just a fancy word for a summary like a sum, a count, or an average. Writing three windows at once means one line of code produces three related features, without anyone copying and pasting a query three times. Two settings do the heavy lifting. Accuracy set to TEMPORAL means "compute the value as of the exact prediction moment," not a stale snapshot from last midnight. And online set to True means this feature is meant to be served live, not just used in training. Those windows are not decoration; each one is a hypothesis about how far back customer behavior actually matters.

This grid shows the feature family we just defined: each signal, like purchase sum or order count, measured across the 7, 30, and 90 day windows. A filled dot means we build that combination; an empty dot means we chose not to. The teaching point is that every dot is a deliberate choice, not a default to switch all on. Each extra window costs more computation, more storage, and a slightly slower lookup. Worse, and this surprises people, the more windows you try, the more likely one of them looks predictive purely by luck, the way flipping enough coins eventually gives a long streak of heads. So add a window because you have a reason to believe that time span matters, and be ready to drop the ones that never earn their keep.

v1 = Join(
    left=prediction_events,

        JoinPart(
            group_by=purchases_v1,

            group_by=cancellations_v1,
            prefix="cancel"),
    ],

The third code step ties it all together. A Join takes the list of decisions we want to score, the "left" side, and attaches the computed features to each one, evaluated as of that row's own timestamp. The card on the left is the key idea: the left side sets the time. Each row carries a customer_id to look up and a ts that says the exact moment to look, so every row gets features frozen at its own cutoff. The right_parts are the feature groups we bolt on, here purchases and cancellations, and the prefix just keeps their column names from colliding. The check_consistency flag turns on the safety net we will see next: Chronon keeps the records needed to compare the live and historical paths and confirm they agree.
One definition, two paths

You can measure whether both paths agree.

04 · Deliver
Two stores hold the same features in different shapes. The offline store keeps the full history and is arranged for big scans during training. The online store keeps only current values and is arranged to return one key very fast. Saying "they should match" is a wish; asking both for the same customer at the same cutoff and comparing the answers is a test. Pin down the key, the cutoff, and the feature name so the only thing that differs is which path answered. The Chronon fixture in this tutorial does exactly that, asserting £30 of prior purchases and £7 of prior cancellations. Without a test like this, a mismatch shows up as a model that looked fine in testing and then makes odd predictions in production.
Consistency test

Compare results, not guesses.

Same key · same cutoff
parity_check.py
offline = backfill(key, cutoff)
online  = replay_fetch(key, cutoff)

assert offline.types == online.types
assert same_nulls(offline, online)
np.testing.assert_allclose(
    offline.values, online.values,
    rtol=1e-6, atol=1e-9)

Values

Equal within a tiny tolerance.

Also test

missing values · types · age

This is the parity check in code, and it is the moment the promise "both paths agree" turns from a hope into a test. We compute the feature two ways for the same key and cutoff: backfill is the historical path, replay_fetch is the live path. Then we assert they match. Point out why the comparison is not a plain equals sign. Computers store decimals with tiny rounding error, so we allow a minuscule tolerance rather than demanding the numbers be identical to the last digit. And matching values is not enough on its own: we also check that the data types agree and that a missing value on one side is missing on the other, because a zero and a blank mean very different things. A test that has never been able to fail is not really protecting you, so it is worth deliberately breaking a value once to confirm the check catches it.

This is the payoff experiment, and it is designed like a fair science experiment: change one thing and hold everything else constant. Both sides use the same model and the same time-based split, where we train on earlier data and test on later data, exactly as real life forces us to. The only difference is the features. The left side uses honest point-in-time features, using only history before the cutoff, and it earns a believable score, clearly better than guessing but far from perfect. The right side sneaks in a feature secretly built from the future, and it scores almost perfectly. The lesson students should carry away is counterintuitive: a suspiciously high score is not a trophy, it is a warning siren that information from the future has leaked into your inputs.
Production meaning

A feature needs clear rules and an owner.

05 · Govern
A feature contract is the written rules for a feature: what it means, which key it is looked up by, its type, its time rule, who owns it, which version it is, how fresh it must be, what a missing value means, and where the numbers came from. That last item is called lineage, the recorded trail from a value back to its sources and the version of the definition that produced it. The freshness promise is a service level objective, a written target such as "99 percent of values readable within 60 seconds". Write all of this down, because the alternative is that the meaning lives only in one engineer's head. The symptom of a missing contract is a pager alert at 2am that nobody can act on, since nobody knows whether zero means "spent nothing" or "we have no data".
Change management

Version the meaning, not the filename.

A break means values changed
SAFE

Improve the docs

MEASURE

Add a feature

The reply gets bigger and slower.

VERSION

Change a window

Old values change too.

Counterintuitive: adding one column can leave every old value correct and still push the lookup past its promised response time.

Features change over time, and how you handle change decides whether models quietly break. The three cards rank changes by risk. Improving the documentation is safe, because no numbers move. Adding a new feature needs measurement, because even though old values stay correct, the reply gets bigger and the lookup slower, and it can blow past your promised response time. Changing a window, say from 30 days to 45, is the dangerous one: it silently changes the meaning of a column that models already depend on. The rule is to version the meaning, not just rename the file. When meaning changes, create a v2, move consumers over deliberately, and retire v1, rather than editing the old feature in place and hoping nothing downstream notices.
Observe outcomes

Fast is not the same as up to date.

Checks at every layer

Freshness · how old the value is

Latency · how long the lookup takes

These two are easy to confuse and they fail in different ways. A lookup can return in two milliseconds and still hand back a number computed six hours ago. Watch both, and watch them at five layers: is the source data arriving, is the calculation running, is the value fresh enough, is delivery meeting its target, and are the model's results still sensible. Latency targets are usually written as p99 latency, meaning the response time that 99 percent of requests come in under, which describes the slow tail rather than the typical case. If you only chart average latency, the slowest 1 percent of your customers, who are often your busiest ones, stay invisible.
Production dataflow

One definition, two different engines.

Chronon at scale 
flowchart LR
  D[Definition] --> S[Stream]
  D --> B[Backfill]
  S --> K[(Online)]
  B --> O[(Offline)]
  K --> F[Serve]
  O --> R[Train]
Read the diagram from the left. One definition splits into two jobs. The stream job reads new events as they arrive and keeps the online store current, so serving has fresh values. The backfill job recomputes historical values from the source history and fills the offline store, so training has a truthful past. They meet again at the definition, which is the whole point: two very different engines, one written meaning. If instead you let the streaming code and the batch code each carry their own copy of the rule, the two will drift apart, and you will only find out when the live model starts behaving unlike the one you tested.
Architecture choice

Only add complexity that pays for itself.

Fit the problem
A feature store is real infrastructure, so it needs a reason. Ask three questions. Do several models or teams need the same features, so that defining them once actually saves work? Does anything have to be answered live, rather than in a nightly batch? Do your features depend on time windows that are easy to get wrong? If the answers are no, a plain scheduled query against your historical tables is the better engineering decision, and choosing it is not a failure. Adopting a platform you do not need costs you real operational work and buys you nothing.

This is the summary checklist, and the four letters spell TDOV. Truth means the point-in-time tests pass, so no future information is leaking backward into the past. Delivery means the training path and the live path produce matching values, and the live one is fast enough to meet its promise. Ownership means every alert has a named human behind it, because a feature nobody owns is a feature nobody fixes. Value means the reuse actually justifies the cost of running the platform. Present these as four gates, all of which must be green before shipping. The closing line is the whole course in one breath: there is one clear rule, stored and delivered in different ways, and your job is to keep checking that what actually reaches the model matches what you intended.
